What is Information?

Information vs Data

Data is raw, unprocessed facts and figures. Information is data that has been processed, organized, and given meaning. For example, "25, 30, 35" is data, but "The temperatures this week were 25°C, 30°C, and 35°C" is information.

Why Information Matters

Information is power. It helps us make decisions, solve problems, learn new things, and communicate with others. In our daily lives, we access and share information constantly:

  • Reading news on a website
  • Watching educational videos on YouTube
  • Sharing photos with friends on social media
  • Searching for information for school projects
  • Sending emails to teachers or classmates

How Search Engines Work

1

Crawl

Search engines use "spiders" or "bots" to explore the web by following links

2

Index

Information about web pages is stored in a massive database

3

Rank

Algorithms determine which pages are most relevant to your search

4

Retrieve

The most relevant results are presented when you search

Search Tips and Tricks

Exact phrase: "climate change" - finds exact match
Exclude word: jaguar -car - excludes results about cars
Site search: site:wikipedia.org "Albert Einstein" - searches only Wikipedia
File type: filetype:pdf "annual report" - finds PDF files
Wildcard: "the * of the matter" - fills in the blank
Date range: 2020..2022 - searches within date range

Offline Information Access

Not all information is accessed online. Offline information access refers to obtaining information without an Internet connection. This is important because not everyone has reliable Internet access, and offline sources can be more reliable and in-depth.

Common Offline Information Sources

Books and Textbooks
Comprehensive, reviewed content
Newspapers and Magazines
Current events, local news
Encyclopedias
General knowledge, reference
Libraries and Archives
Physical collections, historical documents
Educational Materials
Worksheets, manuals, guides
Offline Digital Media
CDs, DVDs, USB drives with stored content

Using an Encyclopedia

  1. Find the right volume: Encyclopedias are organized alphabetically by topic
  2. Use the index: Most encyclopedias have an index volume that lists all topics
  3. Look for cross-references: Articles often reference related topics
  4. Check publication date: Older encyclopedias may not have current information
  5. Take notes: Since you can't copy and paste, write down important information

Evaluating Information Credibility

The CRAAP Test

Use this framework to evaluate information sources:

  • C - Currency: When was the information published or updated?
  • R - Relevance: Does the information relate to your topic?
  • A - Authority: Who is the author/publisher? Are they credible?
  • A - Accuracy: Is the information supported by evidence? Can it be verified?
  • P - Purpose: Why was this information created? To inform? Persuade? Sell?

Red Flags to Watch For

Red FlagWhat to Check
No author or date Look for "About Us" or "Contact" pages
Poor grammar/spelling Professional sources are proofread
Extreme language "Amazing", "Shocking", "You won't believe"
No sources/citations Factual claims should have evidence
URL ending in .com.co or similar Check for legitimate domain names
Confirmation bias Check multiple sources with different perspectives

Netiquette and Online Safety

What is Netiquette?

Netiquette (network etiquette) is the acceptable code of behavior for communicating online. It gives the do's and don'ts of online communication.

Core Netiquette Rules

  • Be respectful: Treat others as you want to be treated
  • Use appropriate language: Avoid ALL CAPS (shouting) and offensive terms
  • Fact-check before sharing: Don't spread misinformation
  • Respect privacy: Don't share others' personal information without permission
  • Think before you post: Once online, it's hard to remove
  • Give credit: Cite sources and respect copyright
  • Be forgiving: Not everyone is tech-savvy or fluent in your language

Protecting Personal Information

Copyright and Intellectual Property

What is Copyright?

Copyright is a legal right that gives creators control over how their original works are used. It automatically applies to creative works like books, music, software, photos, and videos.

What Copyright Protects

Written works (books, articles, blogs)
Visual arts (paintings, photos, drawings)
Music (songs, compositions, recordings)
Video/film (movies, TV shows, YouTube videos)
Software (programs, apps, games)
Facts and ideas (NOT protected)

Fair Use and Educational Exceptions

When You Can Use Copyrighted Material

  • Education: Using small portions for teaching and learning
  • Criticism and commentary: Reviewing or analyzing a work
  • News reporting: Using excerpts in news stories
  • Parody: Satirizing or commenting on a work
  • Research: Using quotes and excerpts with citation

Important: Always give credit to the original creator, and consider whether your use could harm the market for the original work.

Creative Commons and Open Licenses

Creative Commons (CC) - Licenses that allow creators to share their work with certain permissions (attribution, non-commercial, share-alike)

Your Digital Footprint

What is a Digital Footprint?

Your digital footprint is the trail of data you leave behind when using the Internet. This includes websites you visit, emails you send, social media posts, photos you share, and information others post about you.

Types of Digital Footprints

TypeDescriptionExamples
Active Footprint Data you intentionally share Social media posts, comments, uploaded photos, online reviews
Passive Footprint Data collected without your direct action Search history, location data, browsing habits, cookies

Managing Your Digital Footprint

  • Think before you post - Once online, it's permanent
  • Review privacy settings on all social media accounts
  • Google yourself - See what others can find about you
  • Delete old accounts you no longer use
  • Use strong, unique passwords for each account
  • Be careful what you share - Employers and schools may check your online presence

Activity of Integration

Research and Presentation Project

Your class is researching a local environmental issue. You need to gather information, evaluate sources, create a presentation, and share it with the community.

FINAL
Community Research Project

Requirements:

  1. Topic Selection: Choose a local issue (pollution, waste management, water quality, deforestation)
  2. Information Gathering: Use at least 3 online sources and 2 offline sources
  3. Source Evaluation: Apply the CRAAP test to each source and document your evaluation
  4. Information Organization: Create an outline organizing your findings
  5. Presentation Creation: Create a presentation (slides, poster, or video) with:
    • Introduction to the issue
    • Key facts and statistics
    • Visuals (images, charts, diagrams)

  6. Sharing: Present your findings to the class and share via at least one digital platform
  7. Reflection: Write about what you learned about finding, evaluating, and sharing information
